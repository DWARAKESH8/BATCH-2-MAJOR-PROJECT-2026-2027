Crowd Density Thresholds and Escalation Bands

These bands follow the pedestrian level-of-service tradition established by Fruin and used in
venue design and safety certification worldwide. Densities are stated as persons per square
metre of usable walking surface, measured locally rather than averaged over a whole area: a
courtyard averaging one person per square metre can still contain a lethal pocket at six.

Below 1 person per square metre. Free circulation. People choose their own speed and path.
Routine monitoring only.

1 to 2 persons per square metre. Comfortable movement with minor path adjustment. Normal for a
busy concourse. No intervention required.

2 to 3 persons per square metre. Movement becomes constrained. Walking speed drops noticeably
and overtaking stops. Increase monitoring, confirm alternative routes are open and staffed, and
begin watching the trend rather than the level.

3 to 4 persons per square metre. Restricted movement. Individuals can no longer choose their
own path and begin to move as a group. Contact between people becomes frequent. This is the
band in which to act: meter inflow, open additional routes, and place marshals. Interventions
started here are still effective; interventions started later frequently are not.

4 to 5 persons per square metre. Severely restricted. Involuntary contact is continuous.
Individuals cannot stop, turn, or bend down. A person who falls cannot get up unaided and may
not be seen. Stop inflow immediately and open every available egress.

Above 5 persons per square metre. Critical. Pressure transmits through the crowd rather than
being absorbed, so a push at the back is felt at the front. Shock waves become possible.
Compressive asphyxia becomes possible within roughly thirty seconds in the densest pockets,
and it does not require anyone to fall over. Treat as a major incident.

Important qualifications.
Density alone is not the whole risk. A dense crowd moving coherently in one direction is far
safer than a less dense crowd containing opposing streams or stop-and-go waves. Always read
density together with movement quality.
Measured density is only as good as the camera calibration. An uncalibrated view cannot report
absolute persons per square metre reliably, and figures from one should be treated as relative
indicators of trend, not as absolute values to compare against these bands.
Detection-based counting undercounts in exactly this regime, because bodies occlude one another.
Where the detector reports heavy occlusion, treat the count as a lower bound.
