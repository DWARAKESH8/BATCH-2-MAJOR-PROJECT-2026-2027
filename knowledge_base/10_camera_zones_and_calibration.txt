Camera Zone Definition and Ground-Plane Calibration Procedure

Why calibration matters. A count divided by a guessed area is not a density. Under perspective,
a person forty metres from the camera occupies a small fraction of the pixels of a person five
metres away, so a uniform pixel-to-metre conversion systematically misreports density in the
far field. Absolute persons-per-square-metre figures, and any comparison against published
safety thresholds, are only meaningful once the camera has been calibrated to the ground plane.

Calibration procedure.
1. Identify a rectangle on the ground within the camera view whose real dimensions are known.
   Paving grids, pitch markings, standard barrier spacing, parking bays, and expansion joints
   all work. Measure it if it is not already documented.
2. Record the pixel coordinates of its four corners in the camera image.
3. Record the corresponding real-world coordinates in metres, using any consistent origin.
4. Enter both sets as the calibration for that camera.
5. Verify. Ask the system to report the distance between two other known points in the view. If
   the error exceeds roughly a quarter of a metre, the corner points were misidentified or the
   surface is not flat, and the calibration should be redone rather than accepted.

Recalibrate whenever the camera is moved, refocused, or its zoom is changed. An out-of-date
calibration is more dangerous than none, because it reports confident numbers that are wrong.

Zone definition. Define zones that correspond to how the venue is actually operated, and name
them the way staff name them on the radio. An alert reading "Gate 3 throat" reaches somebody who
can act; an alert reading "central image region" does not.

Assign each zone a type, because the type determines which procedure applies:
- gate: an aperture where arrival rate can exceed discharge rate
- exit: a discharge point out of the monitored area
- corridor: a through-route with a dominant direction of travel
- stage: an area with an attraction drawing people forward
- area: general standing or circulation space

Record a design capacity for each zone in persons per square metre. For a gate throat this is
usually lower than for open standing space, because the consequences of reaching it are worse.

Blind spots. Record which parts of the venue no camera covers, and treat that list as part of
the safety plan. Every measurement the system reports is conditional on the area being in view,
and the most dangerous place in a venue is frequently the one nobody is looking at.
