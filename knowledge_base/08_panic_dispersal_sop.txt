Panic Dispersal and Escape Behaviour Standard Operating Procedure

What it is. A crowd moving away from a perceived threat at speed and in scattering directions.
Density typically falls rather than rises. The injury profile is falls, trampling and crush
against fixed obstacles, not compressive asphyxia in the open.

This is a fundamentally different failure from a crush, and several of the correct responses to
a crush are actively harmful here. Metering flow, narrowing an exit or holding people back are
appropriate for congestion and dangerous during a dispersal.

Recognition. Mean movement speed rises sharply above the site baseline, often to running pace.
Directions scatter rather than aligning. Density falls in the origin area and rises abruptly at
exits and at any fixed obstruction. The onset is sudden, unlike congestion which builds.

Immediate actions, in order.
1. Identify and neutralise the trigger. Dispersals have a cause: a loud noise, a fight, smoke,
   a rumour, a barrier collapse, a firework. Removing or explaining the cause ends the event
   faster than any crowd-management measure.
2. Open every exit fully and immediately. Do not meter, do not narrow, do not check tickets.
3. Clear trip hazards from the direction of travel. Falls in the flow line are the principal
   injury mechanism, and each fall creates a secondary obstruction.
4. Post marshals to pull fallen people sideways out of the flow, not to stand in it.
5. Broadcast a calm, factual announcement naming the safe direction and stating that there is
   room for everyone. Silence increases panic; vague reassurance does not help. If the threat
   is not real, say so plainly and say what the noise or sight actually was.
6. Watch the exits and any fixed obstruction. Casualties in a dispersal occur where fast-moving
   people meet something that does not move.

Prohibited actions. Do not close or narrow exits to control the flow: that converts a stampede
into a crush against a fixed barrier, which is far more lethal than the dispersal itself. Do not
send responders against the flow. Do not use the word "panic" in any announcement.

Aftermath. Escape behaviour often reverses within a minute once the trigger is understood.
Prepare for the returning flow, which will meet people still leaving and produce a counter-flow
in the same corridor. Have a one-way plan ready before that happens.
