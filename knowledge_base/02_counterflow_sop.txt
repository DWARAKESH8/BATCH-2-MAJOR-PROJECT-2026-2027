Counter-Flow and Opposing Stream Standard Operating Procedure

Scope. Applies wherever two groups need to move in opposing directions through shared space:
concourses at changeover, corridors serving both an arriving and a departing crowd, shared
stairways, and any route where people turn back after finding an exit closed.

Why it matters. Opposing streams do not simply share a corridor. They interlock. Each stream
obstructs the other, individual walking speed falls, hesitation and side-stepping increase, and
throughput in both directions collapses well below what either would achieve alone. Density
then rises from both ends at once, which is why counter-flow so often appears in the sequence
preceding a crush rather than as an isolated problem.

Recognition. The diagnostic sign is people making progress in two opposite directions along the
same axis, not merely people looking disordered. A jammed crowd also looks chaotic while going
nowhere, and it requires the opposite response, so the two must be told apart before acting.
Ask: are individuals actually arriving somewhere, in two directions? If yes, it is counter-flow.

Immediate actions, in order.
1. Impose one-way movement and announce the direction clearly before density rises. This is
   cheap now and physically impossible once the corridor is packed.
2. Physically separate the streams with line barriers or a marshal line. A painted line or a
   verbal instruction is not sufficient once people are under pressure.
3. Divert the minority stream to an alternative route entirely. Interleaving two streams
   through one space is almost always worse than sending one of them somewhere else.
4. Identify why the opposing stream exists. People rarely walk against a crowd without a
   reason: a closed exit, an unmarked route, a missed turn, a re-entry policy. Removing the
   cause removes the counter-flow.
5. Post marshals at the point where the streams meet, not at the ends of the corridor.

Prohibited actions. Do not attempt to reverse a stream that is already dense and slow. If the
crowd has stopped moving, treat it as a blockage: reversing people inside a jam creates
opposing pressure within the jam itself, which is exactly the mechanism that causes falls.

Announcements. Keep them short, name the direction, name the alternative route, and repeat the
same wording. Conflicting or changing instructions increase hesitation, and hesitation in a
dense crowd is what converts congestion into stoppage.
