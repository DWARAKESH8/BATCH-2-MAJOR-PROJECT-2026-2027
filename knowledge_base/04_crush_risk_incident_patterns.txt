Crowd Crush: Incident Patterns and Early Warning Signs

The common sequence. Reviews of crowd-crush incidents repeatedly show the same progression
rather than a single sudden failure:
1. A local blockage or constriction appears, often small and often removable.
2. Arrivals continue at the previous rate because nobody upstream knows about it.
3. Density rises behind the blockage while throughput through it falls.
4. Route confusion or a closed exit generates a counter-flow inside the queue.
5. Pushing from the rear begins, transmitted forward through bodies as pressure waves.
6. Someone falls; the space they occupied closes; others fall into it.
7. Recognition by the control room comes late, because each individual camera view looked
   merely busy rather than obviously dangerous.

Step seven is the one worth dwelling on. In almost every case there was video, and there were
people watching it. What was missing was measurement: nobody could say that density had gone
from 2.8 to 4.1 in ninety seconds, because nobody was computing it.

Early warning signs, in rough order of appearance.
- Throughput falling while the queue behind grows. The earliest reliable sign.
- A sudden drop in average walking speed with no change in the number of people.
- Repeated backward or lateral movement in a crowd that is supposed to be advancing.
- Stop-and-go waves: the crowd surges, stalls, surges again, at intervals of a few seconds.
- People clustering at a narrow point rather than passing through it.
- Raised voices, arms raised above the crowd, objects held overhead.
- Requests to staff from within the crowd, which frequently precede formal recognition.

Physical facts that shape the response.
Relief must come from the front of a crush. Pressure accumulates from behind, so extraction or
barrier release at the rear achieves nothing and adds delay.
Compressive asphyxia does not require anyone to fall and does not require panic. It occurs in
crowds that are entirely calm and cooperative.
The dangerous variable is pressure, not density alone: local density multiplied by the variance
of local velocities. Turbulent, irregular motion at moderate density is more dangerous than
uniform motion at higher density.
Once turbulence begins, the situation can progress from uncomfortable to fatal in well under a
minute, which is shorter than the time any physical intervention takes to have effect. This is
why acting on the trend rather than the level is the whole of the discipline.
